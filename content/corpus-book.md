# Corpus · Past futures of the book

How and why the Case 1 corpus was scoped and built.

## Scope

Speculations that a medium will transform or replace the book, reading, teaching or the school, from 1878 to the present, in any format. Excluded: speculations about media with no bearing on learning or reading, and works that cannot be quoted or described from a document, such as installations, games and live performances.

**Media:** stereograph · phonograph · film · radio · television · teaching machine · computer · virtual reality · internet · AI · robot · game · direct transmission to the brain.

## Rationale

The corpus speculates about speculative design: it sets speculative design beside the practices around it (predictions, fiction, film, images, reports and design projects) to see how futures of one medium have been imagined across genres and over time. It is therefore built for range rather than representativeness: a spread of formats and periods, read through one codebook that formalises a speculation by what it speculates about.

## How it was developed

The academic speculative design papers are the one strand that can be retrieved systematically, so they come from a stated search; the other strands are selected purposively, and their bias is reported under Limitation.

**Pilot (31 speculations; 28 after one per speaker per medium).** Assembled purposively, not by systematic search: claims quoted in histories of educational technology [1], [2] and traced, where possible, to the press, broadcast and archival sources they cite; widely discussed fiction, film and images; and scenario and speculative design work on education (e.g. Bayne 2015; Selwyn et al. 2020; OECD 2020). Each claim records its source, and a to-verify flag where the wording comes only from a secondary source.

**Extension (ACM Digital Library).** I built a corpus of speculative design papers on education through searches of the ACM Digital Library, run on 22 October 2026. Six searches pair a method term with an education term, both in the title or both in the abstract:

1. “speculative design” and “education”
2. “speculative design” and “classroom”
3. “speculative design” and “school”
4. “design fiction” and “education”
5. “design fiction” and “classroom”
6. “design fiction” and “school”

Each result was screened on its abstract, and where needed its full text, against three criteria: (1) is education, learning or the school the primary issue the paper addresses? (2) is speculative design, or a related practice such as design fiction, the primary research method, according to the authors? (3) does the paper contain at least one speculation in which a medium transforms or replaces the book, reading, teaching or the school? The third held every paper to the case’s own scope: a paper that uses speculative design in a classroom, but whose speculations concern something else, was excluded, while a paper on another topic was included for any speculation that met it, and only that speculation was coded. Included papers were then split into project papers, which present specific speculations, and programmatic papers on the role of speculative design in education; only project papers were coded. The screening is recorded in a screening sheet kept with the project.

**Result.** The six searches returned 64 records, 48 after removing duplicates. Screening excluded 40: 14 by paper type (workshop proposals, doctoral consortium papers, an editorial, an installation and a proceedings volume), 14 where education was the setting rather than the subject, 6 that mentioned education only in passing, 3 that were not speculative, 2 with no medium transforming learning, teaching or reading, and 1 on another primary issue. 8 papers were included, all project papers. Papers and speculations are counted separately, since one paper can hold several speculations: from these 8 papers I coded up to three of each paper’s most developed speculations, 15 in all, from 7 papers. One included paper (Lehnert et al. 2026) yields none, because its design fictions were generated by a language model; and two papers by Sebastian et al. (2026) draw on the same school workshops in New Delhi. Neither says whether the same children speak in both, so their speakers are treated as distinct; on that basis all 15 count under the one-per-speaker-per-work rule (see the codebook).

**Second purposive round (October 2026).** Nineteen records, 1920 to 2025, were added (18 counted speculations and one repeat) from two kinds of source. One is Paleofuture, Matt Novak’s long-running archive of past predictions, which reproduces press items, Sunday comics, children’s books, magazine features and corporate films; each is cited through Paleofuture until checked against the original. The others are recent sources on AI and schooling found alongside them (Alpha School’s TimeBack, 2025). Paleofuture is itself a purposive collection of striking predictions, so this round adds to the lean described under Limitation.

## Recording

Each speculation is coded in a coding sheet whose values are limited to the codebook’s, and each source has a note in the project’s source library holding its speculations, coding notes and citation. The site publishes each speculation’s quotation, codes and citation.

## Limitation

Apart from the academic papers, which come from a systematic search of the ACM Digital Library, the corpus is selected purposively. It leans towards well-known, mostly American sources, and the histories and collections it draws on (among them Paleofuture) favour claims that were dramatic and turned out wrong. The systematic strand is meant to correct this; the aim for later rounds is at least 20 speculations per format, drawn from named source lists by a stated rule.
