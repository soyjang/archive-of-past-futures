Scope

Speculations that a medium will transform or replace the book, reading, teaching or the school, from 1906 to the present, in any format. Excluded: speculations about media with no bearing on learning or reading, and works that cannot be quoted or described from a document, such as installations, games and live performances.

Media: stereograph · film · radio · television · teaching machine · computer · virtual reality · internet · AI · robot · game · direct transmission to the brain. For colour, they are shown in eight families: stereograph and film · radio and television · teaching machines · computers · internet · AI and robots · VR and direct transmission · games.

How it was developed

Pilot (31 speculations; 27 after one per speaker per work). Assembled purposively, not by systematic search: claims quoted in histories of educational technology (Cuban 1986; Watters 2021) and traced, where possible, to the press, broadcast and archival sources they cite; widely discussed fiction, film and images; and scenario and speculative design work on education (e.g. Bayne 2015; Selwyn et al. 2020; OECD 2020). Each claim records its source, and a to verify flag where the wording comes only from a secondary source.

Extension (ACM Digital Library). I built a corpus of speculative design papers on education through searches of the ACM Digital Library, run on 22 October 2026. Six searches pair a method term with an education term, both in the title or both in the abstract:

  1. “speculative design” and “education”
  2. “speculative design” and “classroom”
  3. “speculative design” and “school”
  4. “design fiction” and “education”
  5. “design fiction” and “classroom”
  6. “design fiction” and “school”

Each result was screened on its abstract, and where needed its full text, against three criteria: (1) is education, learning or the school the primary issue the paper addresses? (2) is speculative design, or a related practice such as design fiction, the primary research method, according to the authors? (3) does the paper contain at least one speculation in which a medium transforms or replaces the book, reading, teaching or the school? The third held every paper to the case’s own scope: a paper that uses speculative design in a classroom, but whose speculations concern something else, was excluded, while a paper on another topic was included for any speculation that met it, and only that speculation was coded. Included papers were then split into project papers, which present specific speculations, and programmatic papers on the role of speculative design in education; only project papers were coded.

Result (22 October 2026). The six searches returned 64 records, 48 after removing duplicates. Screening excluded 40: 14 by paper type (workshop proposals, doctoral consortium papers, an editorial, an installation and a proceedings volume), 14 where education was the setting rather than the subject, 6 that mentioned education only in passing, 3 that were not speculative, 2 with no medium transforming learning, teaching or reading, and 1 on another primary issue. 8 papers were included, all project papers. Papers and speculations are counted separately, since one paper can hold several speculations: from these 8 papers I coded up to three of each paper’s most developed speculations, 15 in all, from 7 papers. One included paper (Lehnert et al. 2026) yields none, because its design fictions were generated by a language model; and two papers report the same workshops in India, so their speculations are kept distinct. All 15 count under the one-per-speaker-per-work rule (see the codebook), since no extension speaker appears in two works. The archive now counts 42 speculations: 27 from the pilot and 15 from the extension, with 4 repeats by Edison and Gates kept aside.

    Limitation

    A purposive pilot leans towards well-known, mostly American sources; the extension and its stated search procedure are meant to correct this.
