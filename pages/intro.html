Introduction

Every new medium has been promised as the one that would replace the book in education, from Edison’s films in 1913 to AI tutors today; the climate has been imagined for even longer, from ecological fiction of the 1880s to net-zero scenarios. Many of these futures are now past for us, which makes it possible to read them against what actually happened.

Speculative design imagines futures too, not to predict them but to question the present (Dunne & Raby 2013). Yet it is said to resemble the corporate and policy futures it sets out to critique (Wong & Khovanskaya 2018). And it comes late to a much longer tradition of imagining futures, one that spans discourses, genres and practices, from futures studies and policy reports to science fiction novels and films and earlier design movements, across more than a century. So far, some studies treat speculative design on its own, offering evaluative frames (Baumer et al. 2020), lists of qualities (Ringfort-Felner et al. 2025) and reviews of its papers (Soden et al. 2021b; Sanchez et al. 2025); others set it beside corporate futures (Wong & Khovanskaya 2018; Jang & Bolter 2026), science fiction (Kirby 2010; Bleecker 2009) or futures studies (Candy & Dunagan 2017), but to position speculative design, not to read its futures and theirs on the same terms. How does speculative design imagine differently from these other futures, and where do they converge?

In a sense, the project speculates about speculative design itself. I propose a framework for reading a speculation (a codebook) and read the two cases through it. From the patterns they show, I then look for ways to evaluate speculative design and to imagine it otherwise, through a set of design prompts to be developed into games. The cases are bounded corpora, so these patterns are claims about the cases, to be tested elsewhere.

Approach

Each speculation is read in two ways. McLuhan’s Tetrad asks what it imagines a new medium will enhance, obsolesce, retrieve and reverse. Speculative distance asks how far it departs from its own present: the scope of change it claims, plus the four dimensions of psychological distance in construal-level theory (Trope & Liberman 2010). Construal level is coded separately, to test the theory’s prediction that distant futures are construed abstractly.

This is historicising in Soden et al.’s (2021a) sense: every speculation, speculative design included, is read in the context of when it was made, against the media, institutions and concerns of its time.

Two cases

Equal in weight, read separately, then compared. Together they span academic papers, reports, science fiction, design projects, film and images, from the 1900s to the present.
