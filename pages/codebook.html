Unit of analysis

A speculation is a documented statement or work, made at a datable moment, that imagines a medium changing the world. It may be a prediction or an imagined world, in any format, and it may look forward to a future, back to a past that could have been, or sideways to a parallel present. Each speculation is one record in the archive. A source may contain several, as when an OECD report sets out four scenarios for schooling; each becomes its own record. What counts as relevant to each case is set out with its corpus.

  • Include when the speculation imagines a world other than its own present (a future, a counterfactual past or a parallel present), is attributable to a named person, organisation or work, is dated, and meets the case’s criteria.
  • One per speaker per medium. A speaker who repeats a view about the same medium across works (interviews, talks, articles) counts once for that medium in a case, so that a prolific speaker does not weigh more than the rest: keep the earliest speculation on that medium and keep the others as repeats, outside every count and reading. A speculation about a different medium is a new speculation, not a repeat. Repeats are coded in full and kept as threads: read in date order with the speculation they repeat, they show how one speaker’s speculation changes over time. Workshop participants are one speaker per group where the paper names groups, otherwise one per participant.
  • Up to three per work. Distinct speculations within one work (a report’s scenarios, a design project’s concept cards, the statements of one workshop) each count, up to three per work: those the source develops most fully (described at greatest length, shown as an artefact, or singled out by the authors), preferring different media where more than three qualify.
  • Exclude descriptions of what a medium already does, and unattributed paraphrases. Works that cannot be quoted or described from a document, such as installations, games and live performances, are excluded for now and reported as a limitation.
  • The archive records what speculations imagine, not what happened; outcomes are not coded.

Attributes

Descriptive facts about the speculation and what it imagines, recorded rather than interpreted. Some follow a decision rule: speaker, format and medium status.

Year
When the speculation was made or first published.
Attribution
The named person, organisation or work the speculation comes from (Thomas Edison; Corning, A Day Made of Glass 2).
Speaker
Inventor (promotes a medium they created) · Company (speaks for a firm with a stake in it) · Policymaker (government or regulator) · Intergovernmental body (the IPCC, the OECD) · Activist or NGO · Educator (teacher, administrator, school broadcaster) · Researcher (academic or laboratory) · Designer · Writer or artist · Workshop participants (students, teachers, experts or other stakeholders speculating in a workshop, interview or study run by others). Code the role in which the person spoke, not their best-known role. Code Designer when the speculation is presented as design work (a design fiction, an artefact, a design project); Writer or artist for novels, films and art not presented as design; Researcher when it is presented as a finding or an argument. Follow the authors’ own framing: fiction written as a research method and named as such, such as “social science fiction”, is Researcher; it becomes Designer only when presented as design. Code Workshop participants whenever the speculation is made by the people a study engages rather than by its authors, whatever their profession; the paper is the source.
Format
The form in which the speculation was first made, not where it was found (an Edison interview quoted by Cuban is press): Press or magazine · Talk or broadcast (speech, lecture, keynote, broadcast interview, statement in a legislature) · Book · Academic paper · Report (policy, technical, corporate or intergovernmental) · Novel or story · Film or television · Image (illustration, postcard, poster) · Design project (artefact, prototype, bot). Format is descriptive. Whether a work functions as speculative design is a question for the analysis, not a code. For a design project reported in an academic paper, code Design project when the speculation is the designed work, and Academic paper when it is the paper’s own argument. A workshop participant’s artefact (a prototype, a comic, a design-fiction story) is a Design project; something a participant says, first documented in the paper, is an Academic paper.
Source
Full citation of the original; where quoted via a compilation, cite both. “N/A” when none is known. Flag to verify when the wording or venue comes only from a secondary source.
Text
The passage that carries the speculation, quoted. Where none does, as with an image or a whole novel, a one-sentence description, shown in italics and never in quotation marks. When a paper reports what its participants imagined, quote the participants’ own words or describe their artefact, not the authors’ interpretation of it; the authors’ reading can go in the notes. Passages are selected for relevance by the coder, and every code is read from the selected passage.
Direction
Forward (a future) · Backward (a counterfactual past: what if something had happened otherwise) · Sideways (a parallel present). Most speculations look forward; counterfactual and alternate-history work, in fiction and in speculative design, looks back.
Medium
The medium the speculation promotes or imagines. Medium is meant in McLuhan’s broad sense: any extension of human capacities, from a device or infrastructure to a practice, a style or a policy. Each case has a fixed list, set out with its corpus; the source’s own wording is kept as a detail (television: parlour walls). If a speculation involves several media, code the one it centres on and name the others in the notes. Where the imagined change does not centre on a device, as often in Case 2, the medium is whatever it centres on: a technology, a practice or a policy (carbon capture, a plant-based diet, a carbon tax).
Medium status
Whether the medium, as the speculation describes it being used, existed at the date of the speculation. The judgement is about the described use, not the component technology. Decide in two steps:
  1. Did a working version that does what the speculation describes, for the use it describes, exist at the date? If not, Imagined, even if its parts existed.
  2. If so, was it in use beyond its makers: sold, broadcast, adopted by institutions or the public? If yes, Existing; if it ran only as its makers’ prototype, trial, pilot or demonstration, Emerging.
Examples: Keystone stereographs sold to schools (1906) are existing; Skinner’s machines in his own Harvard trials (1958) are emerging; Bradbury’s parlour walls (1953) are imagined, although broadcast television existed. For backward and sideways speculation, judge status at the time the imagined world is set: television existed in the 1960s, so a speculation set then, in which it replaced the book, concerns an existing medium. Status separates speculation about an existing medium from speculation that is itself a speculative medium.
Notes
Context, and decisions a later coder needs.

Codes

Interpretive judgements, applied with the rules below and checked by a second coder. Each speculation is coded on three: the Tetrad, speculative distance and construal level.

1. Tetrad McLuhan & McLuhan 1988

What the speculation says the medium will do, read through McLuhan’s four laws of media. Code every law the speculation states or implies; most carry one or two.

Enhances
Extends or intensifies an existing capacity. Television can extend the influence of the most talented teachers. (RCA, 1959)
Obsolesces
Pushes an existing medium or practice out of use. Textbooks as the principal medium of teaching will be as obsolete as the horse and carriage. (Edison, 1925)
Retrieves
Brings back something older that had been lost. …what Philip of Macedon’s son Alexander enjoyed as a royal prerogative: the personal services of a tutor… (Suppes, 1966)
Reverses
Pushed to its limit, flips into its opposite. Individual teaching by machine turns into isolation and longing for the old school. (Isaac Asimov, “The Fun They Had”, 1951)

Rule. Code what is promised, not what the coder thinks would follow. For each law coded, name its object in a few words drawn from the speculation: what is enhanced, obsolesced, retrieved or reversed (Edison 1913 obsolesces “books in the public schools”). A denial is not coded as the law it denies: a speculation that a medium will not replace teachers, or will not end car travel, is not obsolescence.

2. Speculative distance

How far the speculation departs from its own present, coded on five dimensions. The first, scope of change, is how deep the change goes, how much of an existing practice or institution the speculation alters: the dimension construal-level theory lacks, and the one that matters most for obsolescence. The other four are the dimensions of psychological distance in construal-level theory (Trope & Liberman 2010): temporal, spatial, social and hypotheticality, each read against the speaker’s own here and now. They are coded as the speculation marks them in its text, since psychological distance proper is a person’s sense of distance and cannot be read from a document. Scope is primary: crossed with the Tetrad, it forms the wheel. The other four are reported descriptively.

2a. Scope of change

Supplements
Adds alongside an existing practice. The stereograph may be used advantageously either with or without a textbook. (Eliot, 1906)
Transforms practice
Changes how a practice is done, without displacing a medium. The role of the teacher may well be changed… (Skinner, 1958)
Replaces a medium
Displaces an existing medium or system: the textbook, coal power, the private car. …it will supplant largely, if not entirely, the use of textbooks. (Edison, 1922)
Replaces an institution
Displaces an institution or a way of life: the school, the fossil economy. There won’t be schools in the future. (Papert, 1984)

Rule. Code the largest change the speculation asserts.

2b. Temporal distance

The horizon the speculation states, with the signalling phrase recorded. Most give no year, so this is an ordinal code, not a number of years.

Imminent
Up to about five years. soon, in a few years, the next 18 months, at the cusp.
Near
About five to fifteen years. inside of ten years, over the next decade, in the next 10 to 15 years.
Distant
Beyond about fifteen years. fifty years from now, by 2100.
Unstated
No horizon given, or only a vague one (in the future). A value in its own right: a vague horizon may itself mark abstract construal.

Rules. If a year is given, map it by the thresholds. If two phrases conflict, code the more specific (Edison 1913: soon and inside of ten years → near). For fiction and scenarios, use the setting year if one is given (Asimov’s “The Fun They Had”, written in 1951 and set in 2157 → distant). In every direction, measure from the speaker’s present to when the imagined world is set. Backward: a 2020 work set in a 1960s where television replaced the book → distant, reading the levels as distances into the past. Sideways: a parallel present is set now → imminent; its distance lies in hypotheticality, not in time. Thresholds are provisional.

2c. Spatial distance

How far the setting lies from the speaker’s own place.

Here
The speaker’s own place, at any size: their school, city or country (our educational system).
Elsewhere
A place that is not the speaker’s own: another country or region, another planet.
Unspecified
No particular place, or all places at once (in the whole world). Kept off the near-to-far scale: a future set nowhere in particular is not far, only unplaced.

Rule. Fiction is read from its author’s place: an American story set in a future American home is here; a school on Vulcan is elsewhere.

2d. Social distance

How far the people the speculation concerns are from the speaker and audience.

Us
The speaker’s or audience’s own community (your children, our pupils).
Others
A group unlike the speaker and audience: a named group (children who might otherwise fail), future generations, or non-humans, with the group named in the notes.
Unspecified
No particular group (humanity, learners everywhere). Kept off the near-to-far scale, as for place.

Rule. Code Us whenever the group is the speaker’s or audience’s own, however large (Suppes’s millions of schoolchildren are American schoolchildren, his audience’s own); Unspecified only when no group is singled out.

2e. Hypotheticality

How far the speculation stands from what its speaker takes to be real. Construal-level theory treats likelihood as a distance: an event held certain is near, one held possible is further, and one only depicted in an imagined world is furthest. The codes follow that scale.

Certain
The nearest. Stated as fact or certainty: will, is destined to, present-tense assertion.
Possible
Further. Stated as possibility: can, could, may, conditional would. Scenarios offered as one possibility among several also code here.
Depicted
The furthest. Written as if it already is, within an imagined world, as in fiction, illustration or a scenario vignette.

Rule. Code the main verb of the predictive clause; hedges in surrounding text do not change it. A wish or a call for change (should, must, I want) is coded by the likelihood its wording gives the change, and as Possible when it gives none.

3. Construal level Trope & Liberman 2010

The kind of description, coded as what distance is expected to produce, so the archive can test whether far speculations were written abstractly. Shown on the wheel as filled (concrete) or hollow (abstract) dots.

Concrete
How and with what: objects, procedures, settings. With a large-screen receiver in every room… (Hennock, 1955)
Abstract
Why and what for: values, ends, transformations. …the biggest positive transformation that education has ever seen. (Khan, 2023)

Rule. Code the dominant register of the work itself: the quoted passage, or for an image, film or novel the work as seen or read, never the one-sentence description written for the archive.

Worked example

One speculation coded on every attribute and code, with the reasoning for each decision. It is Edison’s 1922 claim:

“I believe that the motion picture is destined to revolutionize our educational system and that in a few years it will supplant largely, if not entirely, the use of textbooks.”

— Thomas Edison, in Hugh Weir, “The Story of the Motion Picture”, McClure’s, November 1922

Attributes
Year
1922. The date of first publication in McClure’s, not of later quotation (Cuban 1986).
Attribution
Thomas Edison. Named as the speaker in the interview.
Speaker
Inventor. Edison speaks as the promoter of a medium he helped create, not as an educator.
Format
Press or magazine. An interview published in a magazine; where it was found (a book) does not change the format.
Source
Hugh Weir, “The Story of the Motion Picture”, McClure’s 54, November 1922, p. 85; quoted in Cuban (1986). The original is cited first, then the compilation where it was found. To verify: the full wording comes from secondary sources and is checked against McClure’s before the code is final.
Text
Quoted, as above: the two clauses that carry the speculation.
Direction
Forward. A claim about what film will do.
Medium
Film. “The motion picture”.
Medium status
Existing. Films had been made and screened for more than two decades by 1922, and some schools already used them. Edison speculates about what an existing medium will do, not about an invented one.
Codes
1. Tetrad
Laws
Enhances, Obsolesces. “Revolutionize our educational system” promises enhancement; “supplant … the use of textbooks” names what becomes obsolete. Nothing is said about what film would bring back or turn into, so retrieval and reversal are not coded, however tempting they are in hindsight.
2. Speculative distance
Scope
Replaces a medium. The textbook is displaced; the school is not. “Revolutionize our educational system” is rhetoric about the system, but the change asserted is to its medium. Rule: code the largest change the speculation asserts, not its most dramatic word.
Temporal
Imminent (“in a few years”). An explicit horizon of up to about five years.
Spatial
Here. “Our educational system”: the speaker’s own country.
Social
Us. The pupils of “our” system; no other group is singled out.
Hypotheticality
Certain. “Is destined to” and “will supplant” state certainty. “I believe” frames the claim but does not hedge the main verb.
3. Construal level
Level
Abstract. The speculation names an end (a revolution, the textbook’s disappearance) and no means: no projectors, rooms, teachers or costs.

Reliability

All codes in this version are by one coder. A second coder will code a sample of each case after reading this codebook, for the Tetrad, speculative distance and construal level. Disagreements are discussed until resolved, and the codebook is revised where they cluster. Agreement is not measured statistically: the aim is a shared reading and a sharper codebook, not a reliability score (McDonald et al. 2019).
