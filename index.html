Historicising Speculative Design

Overview Codebook 1 · Past futures of the book 2 · Climate futures From readings to design

Overview

Every new medium has been promised as the one that would replace the book in education, from Edison’s films in 1913 to AI tutors today; the climate has been imagined for even longer, from ecological fiction of the 1880s to net-zero scenarios. Such futures shape what is funded and built, and speculative design sets out to question them. But does it imagine differently?

The question. Whether speculative design differs from the corporate and policy futures it is said to resemble (Wong & Khovanskaya 2018; Jang & Bolter 2026) or resist (Dunne & Raby 2013) remains untested. And we do not know what a century of such predictions imagined, and what they repeatedly left out. Existing accounts offer evaluative frames (Baumer et al. 2020), lists of qualities (Ringfort-Felner et al. 2025) or reviews of papers (Soden et al. 2021b; Sanchez et al. 2025), not readings of the futures themselves.

The method. To answer, I build a codebook, read the two cases through it, and turn what it reveals into design. The cases are bounded corpora, so the patterns they show are claims about these cases, to be tested elsewhere.

Approach

Each speculation is read in two ways. McLuhan’s Tetrad asks what it imagines a new artefact will enhance, obsolesce, retrieve and reverse. Speculative distance asks how far it departs from its own present: the scope of change it claims, plus the four dimensions of psychological distance in construal-level theory (Trope & Liberman 2010). Construal level is coded separately, to test the theory’s prediction that distant futures are construed abstractly.

This is historicising in Soden et al.’s (2021a) sense: every speculation, speculative design included, is read as a document of its own present. Edison’s 1913 claim is measured from 1913, not from today. The readings then become material for design through play and games. For example, counterfactual obsolescence takes a past prediction at its word and completes it with what it failed to imagine.

      From readings to design

      The readings become material for design through play: game mechanics that let people act on what past speculation imagined and what it left out. Each approach comes from a reading.

      1 · Counterfactual obsolescence

      Taking past futures at their word: design fictions in which a documented prediction of replacement came true, completed with what it failed to imagine.

      1. Select one speculation per medium, by a rule applied to the coding.
      2. Complete the Tetrad. The claim supplies what it promised; the fiction supplies what it left out: what the new medium retrieves and what it reverses into.
      3. Set the distance. Each counterfactual is made near and concrete, and far and abstract.
      4. Watch for remediation. Leave room for viewers to reject the world or to rebuild something book-like within it; either response is a finding.

      The tie-break could equally favour the most recent speculation, giving each medium its present future (for AI, Gates 2025 rather than Stephenson 1995). From the analysis, not the method: promises recur across eras (the personal tutor in 1966, 1995 and 2023), and fiction already imagines many of the retrievals and reversals that forecasts miss. Anachronistic staging, such as Edison and Gates on one panel, remains an option for presenting the fictions. Storyboards are drafts; animations will be made with an AI-assisted multiplane tool.

      Codebook

      How each speculation is selected and coded. Developed on Project 1 and being adapted for Project 2. Version 0.1, provisional: written by one coder from the pilot sample, to be revised after double-coding.

      Unit of analysis

      A speculation is a documented statement or work, made at a datable moment, that imagines a new medium transforming or replacing the book, reading, teaching or the school. It may be a prediction or an imagined world, in any format. A source may yield several speculations; each is coded separately.

      • Include when the speculation is forward-looking or set in a future, attributable to a named person, organisation or work, dated, and concerns the book, reading, teaching or schooling.
      • Exclude descriptions of what a medium already does, unattributed paraphrases, and speculations about media with no bearing on learning or reading.
      • Text. Quote the passage that carries the speculation. Where none does, as with an image or a whole novel, write a one-sentence description, shown in italics and never in quotation marks.
      • The archive records predictions only. What each medium went on to do is read against the claims in the written analysis, not coded here.

      Attributes

      Descriptive facts about the speculation, recorded rather than interpreted.

      Year
      When the speculation was made or first published.
      Medium
      The medium the speculation promotes or imagines, from a fixed list: stereograph · film · radio · television · teaching machine (mechanical or robot teachers) · computer · virtual reality · internet (online courses, platforms) · AI · direct transmission to the brain. The source’s own wording is kept as a detail (television: parlour walls; computer: learning pods). New media are added to the list as they appear. For colour and filtering only, media are shown in seven families: stereograph and film · radio and television · teaching machines · computers · internet · AI · VR and direct transmission.
      Medium status
      Whether the medium, as the speculation describes it being used in education, existed at the date of the speculation. The judgement is about the described use, not the component technology: computers existed in 1971, but not a computer that matched learners by shared interests. Decide in two steps:
      1. Did a working version that does what the speculation describes, for the educational use it describes, exist at the date? If not, Imagined, even if its parts existed.
      2. If so, was it in use beyond its makers: sold, broadcast or adopted by schools or the public? If yes, Existing; if it ran only as its makers’ prototype, trial, pilot or demonstration, Emerging.
      Examples: Keystone stereographs sold to schools (1906) are existing; Skinner’s machines in his own Harvard trials (1958) and the Teacherbot in Bayne’s own MOOC (2015) are emerging; Bradbury’s parlour walls (1953) and Corning’s connected glass (2012) are imagined, although broadcast television and glass both existed. Status separates speculation about an existing medium from speculation that is itself a speculative medium.
      Format
      The form in which the speculation was first made, not where it was found (an Edison interview quoted by Cuban is press): Press or magazine · Talk or broadcast (speech, lecture, keynote, broadcast interview, statement in a legislature) · Book · Academic paper · Report (policy, technical or corporate) · Novel or story · Film or television · Image (illustration, postcard, poster) · Design project (artefact, prototype, bot). Format is descriptive. Whether a work functions as speculative design is a question for the analysis, not a code: a novel or a policy scenario can do the work of speculative design as much as a designed artefact.
      Speaker
      Inventor (promotes a medium they created) · Company (speaks for a firm with a stake in it) · Policymaker (government or regulator) · Educator (teacher, administrator, school broadcaster) · Researcher (academic or laboratory) · Speculative designer · Writer or artist. Code the role in which the person spoke, not their best-known role.
      Source
      Full citation of the original; where quoted via a compilation, cite both. “N/A” when none is known.
      To verify
      Flag when the wording or venue comes only from a secondary source.

      Codes

      Interpretive judgements, applied with the rules below and checked by a second coder. Each speculation is coded on three: the Tetrad, speculative distance and construal level.

      1. Tetrad McLuhan & McLuhan 1988

      What the claim says the new medium will do, read through McLuhan’s four laws of media. Code every law the claim states or clearly implies; most claims carry one or two.

      Enhances
      Extends or intensifies an existing capacity. Television can extend the influence of the most talented teachers. (RCA, 1959)
      Obsolesces
      Pushes an existing medium or practice out of use. Textbooks as the principal medium of teaching will be as obsolete as the horse and carriage. (Edison, 1925)
      Retrieves
      Brings back something older that had been lost. …what Philip of Macedon’s son Alexander enjoyed as a royal prerogative: the personal services of a tutor… (Suppes, 1966)
      Reverses
      Pushed to its limit, flips into its opposite. In the pilot sample it appears in fiction and scenarios, not in interviews or talks: in Asimov’s story, individual machine teaching turns into isolation and longing for the old school.

      Rule. Code what is promised, not what the coder thinks would follow. A denial (“would not replace teachers”) is not coded as obsolescence.

      2. Speculative distance

      How far the claim departs from its own present, coded on five dimensions. The first, scope of change, is how much of the world the claim alters: the dimension construal-level theory lacks, and the one that matters most for obsolescence. The other four are the dimensions of psychological distance in construal-level theory (Trope & Liberman 2010): temporal, spatial, social and hypotheticality. They are coded as the claim marks them in its text, since psychological distance proper is a person’s sense of distance and cannot be read from a document. Scope is primary: crossed with the Tetrad, it forms the wheel. The other four are reported descriptively. Temporal distance, for example, belongs to a claim as a whole rather than to any one law, and most claims state no horizon at all.

      2a. Scope of change

      Supplements
      Adds alongside existing teaching. The stereograph may be used advantageously either with or without a textbook. (Eliot, 1906)
      Transforms practice
      Changes how teaching is done, without displacing a medium. The role of the teacher may well be changed… (Skinner, 1958)
      Replaces the medium
      Displaces the book or another medium of teaching. …it will supplant largely, if not entirely, the use of textbooks. (Edison, 1922)
      Replaces the institution
      Displaces the school or university itself. There won’t be schools in the future. (Papert, 1984)

      Rule. Code the largest change the claim asserts.

      2b. Temporal distance

      The horizon the claim states, with the signalling phrase recorded. Most claims give no year, so this is an ordinal code, not a number of years.

      Imminent
      Up to about five years. soon, in a few years, the next 18 months, at the cusp.
      Near
      About five to fifteen years. inside of ten years, over the next decade, in the next 10 to 15 years.
      Distant
      Beyond about fifteen years. fifty years from now, one day, eventually.
      Unstated
      No horizon given, or only a vague one (in the future). A value in its own right: a vague horizon may itself mark abstract construal.

      Rules. If a year is given, map it by the thresholds. If two phrases conflict, code the more specific (Edison 1913: soon and inside of ten years → near). For fiction and scenarios, use the setting year if one is given (Asimov’s 2157 → distant). Thresholds are provisional.

      2c. Spatial distance

      This classroom
      A single room, school or teacher.
      This country
      A national or regional school system (the public schools, the Nation’s finest teaching personnel).
      Elsewhere
      Another country or region than the speaker’s.
      Worldwide
      Education everywhere (in the whole world).

      2d. Social distance

      Our children
      The speaker’s or audience’s own pupils (your children).
      Other groups
      A named group unlike the audience (children who might otherwise fail, struggling colleges).
      Everyone
      Learners in general (millions of schoolchildren).

      2e. Hypotheticality

      Will
      Stated as fact or certainty: will, is destined to, present-tense assertion.
      Should
      Stated as a goal or obligation: should, must, ought to.
      Could
      Stated as possibility: can, could, may, conditional would. Scenarios offered as one possibility among several also code here.
      Depicted
      Shown as already real within an imagined world, as in fiction, illustration or a scenario vignette.

      Rule. Code the main verb of the predictive clause; hedges in surrounding text do not change it.

      3. Construal level Trope & Liberman 2010

      The kind of description, coded as what distance is expected to produce, so the archive can test whether far claims were written abstractly. Shown on the wheel as filled (concrete) or hollow (abstract) dots.

      Concrete
      How and with what: objects, procedures, settings. With a large-screen receiver in every room… (Hennock, 1955)
      Abstract
      Why and what for: values, ends, transformations. …the biggest positive transformation that education has ever seen. (Khan, 2023)

      Rule. Code the dominant register of the quoted passage. A planned check: score wording against published concreteness norms (Brysbaert et al. 2014).

      Worked example

      One speculation coded on every dimension, with the reasoning for each decision.

      “I believe that the motion picture is destined to revolutionize our educational system and that in a few years it will supplant largely, if not entirely, the use of textbooks.”

      — Thomas Edison, in Hugh Weir, “The Story of the Motion Picture”, McClure’s, November 1922

      Unit of analysis
      Speculation
      One speculation. Forward-looking, attributed, dated, and about the book and schooling. The two clauses make one prediction, so they are coded together.
      Attributes
      Year
      1922. The date of first publication in McClure’s, not of later quotation (Cuban 1986).
      Medium
      Film. “The motion picture”.
      Medium status
      Existing. Films had been made and screened for more than two decades by 1922, and some schools already used them. Edison speculates about what an existing medium will do, not about an invented one.
      Format
      Press or magazine. An interview published in a magazine; where it was found (a book) does not change the format.
      Speaker
      Inventor. Edison speaks as the promoter of a medium he helped create, not as an educator.
      Source
      Hugh Weir, “The Story of the Motion Picture”, McClure’s 54, November 1922, p. 85; quoted in Cuban (1986). The original is cited first, then the compilation where it was found.
      To verify
      Yes. The full wording comes from secondary sources and is checked against McClure’s before the code is final.
      Codes
      1. Tetrad
      Laws
      Enhances, Obsolesces. “Revolutionize our educational system” promises enhancement; “supplant … the use of textbooks” names what becomes obsolete. Nothing is said about what film would bring back or turn into, so retrieval and reversal are not coded, however tempting they are in hindsight.
      2. Speculative distance
      Scope
      Replaces the medium. The textbook is displaced; the school is not. “Revolutionize our educational system” is rhetoric about the system, but the change asserted is to its medium. Rule: code the largest change the claim asserts, not its most dramatic word.
      Temporal
      Imminent (“in a few years”). An explicit horizon of up to about five years.
      Spatial
      This country. “Our educational system”: the national school system of the speaker.
      Social
      Our children. The pupils of “our” system; no other group is singled out.
      Hypothetical
      Will. “Is destined to” and “will supplant” state certainty. “I believe” frames the claim but does not hedge the main verb.
      3. Construal level
      Level
      Abstract. The claim names an end (a revolution, the textbook’s disappearance) and no means: no projectors, rooms, teachers or costs.

      Reliability

      All codes in this version are by one coder. Planned: a second coder codes a 20% sample after training on this codebook; agreement is reported per dimension (Cohen’s κ), and the codebook is revised where disagreement clusters.
